Domain Cutover Runbook

Moving harvardintech.com to the new site

What it takes to point the real domain at the site we've built, in the order it has to happen, with the exact values for whoever sits in the GoDaddy console — and what we need from you before anyone can start.

Plan only · DNS discovery 2026-07-02 · page written 2026-08-05 · steps S1–S9 · decisions D1–D5 · checks V1–V6 · rollback R1–R3

What this actually is

The site is already built. This is only about pointing the address at it.

harvardintech.com is served today by Strikingly, the no-code tool the current site was built in. The replacement site is finished and running — it's the one you've been reviewing. Nothing about it changes at cutover. What changes is a handful of settings at the domain registrar, so that typing harvardintech.com lands on the new site instead of the old one.

The technical work is genuinely small. The care is all in sequencing: doing the reversible parts first, proving each one before the next, and keeping the old site alive underneath the whole time so there is always a way back.

Nothing here has happened yet.

Every step below is written in the future tense on purpose. No DNS record has been read or changed, the site is still gated behind its passphrase, and harvardintech.com still shows the Strikingly site exactly as it did yesterday. This page exists so the move can be reviewed before it is run, not reconstructed afterwards.

The one rule

Email must not break.

@harvardintech.com email runs on HostGator, separately from the website, and it is configured through the same registrar screen we'll be editing. The one action that would do real damage is the tempting one: "replace all records." That would take nadia@harvardintech.com and every other mailbox offline, and mail sent during the outage may simply bounce rather than queue.

Only two things change: the apex A records and the www CNAME. The MX record, the mail. host record, and the SPF TXT record are never touched. Step S7 spells out exactly which lines to edit, and V5 makes proving mail still flows a required step before the cutover is called done — not a thing we check afterwards if someone complains.

What it feels like on the day

Minutes of risk, not hours — and a way back the whole time.
  • The switch itself takes a few minutes. A day beforehand we shorten how long the internet is allowed to remember the old address (from hours to five minutes), so when we do flip it, it takes effect almost immediately instead of trickling out over an afternoon.
  • Some visitors see the old site briefly. During those few minutes, whether someone gets the old or new site depends on their internet provider's cache. Both sites work, so nobody sees an error — some people just see yesterday's page for a moment.
  • The certificate takes a little longer. GitHub issues the HTTPS certificate for the domain after the records point at it — typically minutes, occasionally up to an hour. We wait for it before turning on "Enforce HTTPS", so nobody meets a browser warning.
  • We stage on a real subdomain first. Before the apex flips, the new site runs on review.harvardintech.com with a real certificate, so we've seen it work on the real domain (S2) before anything visitors use is touched.
  • Strikingly stays paid for several days after. Rollback is then just putting the old records back (R1–R3), with nothing to re-create or re-configure. We only cancel once the new site has been stable for a few days (S9).

What we need from you first

Five open questions. The first one blocks everything.

Nothing below is a technical blocker — the work is ready. These are the calls that are yours rather than ours, and D1 in particular gates the whole sequence: without it, S1 can't even be started.

D1

Who holds the GoDaddy login?

Blocks S1

The domain is registered at GoDaddy and its DNS is managed there too — which is good news, because it means no slow registrar transfer is needed. But we've never had access, and this has been an open question since the 2026-07-02 investigation. Nothing can start without it.

  • Someone on the team has it — point us at them and we'll walk them through it screen by screen; they make the edits, we never hold the credentials.
  • Give us access directly — GoDaddy supports delegate access, which can be revoked afterwards and doesn't mean handing over the account password.
  • Nobody knows — then finding out is genuinely the first task, and it may take longer than the rest of this page combined. GoDaddy's account recovery runs on the registrant email on file.

Your input: who manages the domain today, and which of these routes do you want?

D2

Is the content ready to be public?

Blocks S5

Right now the site is behind a passphrase, so placeholders are fine — they read as work in progress. At cutover they stop being drafts and become what the organisation says about itself. The status page's "content to fill or remove" list is the checklist; the open items are three example sponsors rendering as real partners, five board members with no bios, a volunteer project still in draft, and a Give button that opens an email because there's no donation platform yet.

  • Close the list first — fill or remove each item, then cut over. Slower, but nothing embarrassing goes live.
  • Cut over and fix live — the site is editable through the CMS and redeploys in a minute or two, so gaps can be closed after. Faster, but the gaps are public while they're open.
  • Split it — treat the sponsors specifically as blocking (they name organisations that aren't actually partners) and let the rest be fixed live.

Your input: which of these, and if it's "close the list first", who owns each item?

D3

The board design gallery becomes public. Keep, gate, or remove?

Blocks S6

There are thirteen homepage design mockups (about 20MB) published at an unguessable URL for the board to look at. They're hidden from search engines but have no passphrase — the unguessable address is the access mechanism, and it's a link that's actively been shared. The moment the site becomes public, that folder ships to harvardintech.com along with everything else. Anyone who has the link — or is forwarded it — keeps working access to abandoned design directions on the organisation's real domain.

  • Leave it — it's already effectively public and nobody is going to guess the URL. Costs nothing, changes nothing, and the board's link keeps working.
  • Move it to the review site — it stays reachable for the board behind the same passphrase they already use, and never lands on the public domain. Their current link breaks and has to be re-sent.
  • Remove it — the mockups have served their purpose and the chosen direction is built. Their link breaks permanently.

Your input: is the board still using that link? That's really the whole question.

D4

This page and the status page become public too. Where should they live?

Blocks S6

The status page and this runbook are ordinary files inside the site, so they'd ship to the public domain at cutover as well. Their passphrase is a deterrent that runs in the browser — the text is technically in the downloaded page — so on a public domain it protects against casual readers and not much else. What's behind it is internal candour: what isn't built, what we still need from you, and where the domain's records live.

  • Move both to the review site — the gated track exists precisely for this. Your links change once, and they can never leak onto the public domain by accident.
  • Leave them — the passphrase plus an unlisted URL is what's protecting them today, and that stays true after cutover. Simplest, and no links change.
  • Remove them at cutover — they're launch-phase artifacts. Cleanest, but you lose the shared status view exactly when the project moves into its next phase.

Your input: we'd default to moving them, but the link churn is yours to weigh.

D5

When do we cut over?

Scheduling

The switch itself is minutes and can be scheduled tightly, but the day matters for a reason that isn't technical: if something is wrong, we want people available to notice and decide, not to find out on Saturday night.

  • A weekday morning — the whole team is around, everyone can check email straight after, and there's a full working day to react. This is what we'd pick.
  • Right before an event or campaign — worst case; traffic peaks exactly while the change is settling.
  • After a specific milestone — e.g. once the board has signed off, or the content list is closed. Tell us the milestone and we'll work back from it.

Your input: a target window, and who wants to be on the call when it happens.

S2

Prove it on a real subdomain

Not started

Before touching the address people actually use, run the new site on review.harvardintech.com — a real subdomain of the real domain, with a real certificate. If anything is going to behave differently on the live domain than it does in preview, this is where it shows up, while the live site is completely untouched.

Note The gated review track already exists, so this costs one DNS record and no new setup.
Operator detail

This is a simplification over the original 2026-07-02 runbook, which proposed a throwaway new.harvardintech.com. The two-track publishing work has since stood up a permanent gated track, and it is the natural staging target — one subdomain instead of two, and the one we keep afterwards.

  1. Add a single record at GoDaddy: CNAME review → nseldeib.github.io. Nothing about the live site or email is involved.
  2. Set the review repo's Pages custom domain to review.harvardintech.com.
  3. Wait for propagation and GitHub's certificate, then review on the real domain with the team.
S3

Work out where old links should land

Not started

Links to the old site exist in the wild — in emails, on LinkedIn, in other people's posts. Where an old address doesn't match a new one, we catch it and send the visitor to the right page instead of a dead end.

Honest limit This works for people; it's weaker for Google. See the detail.
Operator detail

Crawl the live Strikingly site and build an old-path → new-path table in a new src/lib/redirects.ts, with a pure lookup function so the map is unit-testable and reviewable as data. scripts/extract.mjs already drives Playwright against https://www.harvardintech.com/ and can be adapted to dump the link graph rather than screenshot sections. Paths that map 1:1 (/, /events, /blog) need no entry.

src/pages/404.astro then looks up location.pathname and redirects on a hit, keeping its existing "go back home" markup for misses.

The accepted loss: GitHub Pages cannot serve a server-side 301, so this is a soft redirect — a 200 page that then moves the browser. Humans following an old link land correctly; search engines treat it far more weakly than a 301, so some ranking on changed paths is lost. The alternative is putting Cloudflare in front purely for redirect rules, which is a permanent moving part for a one-time problem. Worth revisiting only if the crawl turns up genuinely high-traffic paths that changed.

S4

Tell the host which domain it's serving

Not started

A one-line file in the site that names the domain. Without it, GitHub serves the site but doesn't know it's supposed to answer to harvardintech.com. Invisible to visitors; nothing changes for anyone until the records move in S7.

Operator detail

New file public/CNAME containing harvardintech.com — the mechanism DEPLOY_SETUP.md "Path A: Custom Domain" already documents.

One ordering trap: it also gets copied into the review track's output. That's harmless provided the review publish step writes its own CNAME (review.harvardintech.com) after the copy, not before. Reversed, the review site would claim the apex domain and the two tracks would fight over it.

S5

Take the passphrase off

Not started

The moment the site stops being private. One configuration change removes the passphrase gate and tells search engines they may now index the site. Worth pausing on: it's the step that can't be quietly undone, because search engines start reading immediately.

Needs D2 answered — this is when placeholders become public.
Operator detail

In .github/workflows/deploy.yml, public track build step only:

  • Drop DEPLOY_BASE_PATH so base → /.
  • Set PAGES_SITE: https://harvardintech.com.
  • Drop PREVIEW_GATE.

astro.config.mjs needs no change — both values are already env-driven and were written for exactly this switch.

Three things follow with no extra work:

  • robots.txt flips from Disallow: / to Allow: / with a correct absolute Sitemap: line, built from the configured site.
  • sitemap-index.xml and llms.txt start emitting the real domain.
  • With base back at /, the CMS admin's root-absolute links resolve correctly again — they're currently broken on the /harvardintech subpath deploy. A real bug fixed as a side effect.

S7

Point the domain at the new site

Not started

The actual switch, and the only step with a risk window. Two settings change; the email settings sitting next to them are left strictly alone. A day beforehand we shorten the caching so this takes effect in minutes.

The rule again Never "replace all records". Edit two lines; leave MX, mail., and every TXT exactly as they are.
Operator detail
  1. A day ahead: lower TTL on the apex A records and the www CNAME to 300. Nothing else changes; this only shortens how long the old answer is remembered.
  2. Repo Settings → Pages: set the custom domain to harvardintech.com.
  3. At GoDaddy, replace the apex A records with GitHub's four: 185.199.108.153, 185.199.109.153, 185.199.110.153, 185.199.111.153. Optionally add the matching AAAA records. Repoint CNAME www → nseldeib.github.io. Leave MX, the mail. host record, and every TXT untouched.
  4. Wait for GitHub to issue the certificate, then enable Enforce HTTPS. Enabling it early is what produces a browser warning.
  5. Run V5 — send a test email both ways — before calling the cutover done.

Checks before we call it done

Six things to confirm. V5 is the one that matters most.
  • V1Both harvardintech.com and www.harvardintech.com serve the new site over HTTPS, with no certificate warning in a fresh browser.
  • V2robots.txt reads Allow: / and points at a Sitemap: URL on the real domain — not the old placeholder, and not the gated track's Disallow.
  • V3sitemap-index.xml lists real harvardintech.com URLs, and llms.txt emits the real origin.
  • V4A sample of old Strikingly URLs from the S3 crawl land somewhere sensible rather than on a bare 404.
  • V5Send a real email to and from an @harvardintech.com address. Both directions, before declaring the cutover done — not after someone reports it. This is the check that catches the one mistake that actually costs something.
  • V6review.harvardintech.com still resolves and is still gated. The public launch must not disturb the review track — and it's easy not to notice that it did, because nobody visits it on cutover day. Also confirm the Search Console verification tag survived; it lives in the CMS "Custom <head> HTML" field, not in the code.

If something's wrong

Putting it back is three values, and they're written here on purpose.

Rollback works because Strikingly stays alive until S9 and because the TTLs were lowered in S7 — so reverting propagates in minutes, the same as the switch did. These are the values as of the 2026-07-02 discovery; S1 re-confirms them, and whatever S1 finds is what belongs here.

  • R1Apex harvardintech.com → A 54.183.102.22 (Strikingly). Remove the four GitHub A records.
  • R2www → CNAME www.harvardintech.com.s.strikinglydns.com.
  • R3Leave MX, mail., and TXT alone — as in the cutover, so they are alone in the rollback. If email has broken, these records are where to look, and the fix is restoring exactly what S1 captured.
